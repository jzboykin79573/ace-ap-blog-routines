## What a 5 Actually Requires {#what-a-5-actually-requires}

A 5 on AP Calculus AB requires earning roughly 68 out of 108 composite points. That is the score you build by consistently answering MCQ questions you know cold and executing FRQ setups clearly enough that a grader following a rubric cannot ignore your work.

Most prep guides will tell you to memorize formulas and do a lot of practice problems. That advice is not wrong. It is just incomplete.

Here is what actually separates the 4s from the 5s in my experience: the ability to move between representations. A 5-scorer can look at a graph, a table, a formula, or a word problem and pull the same information out of all four. A 4-scorer usually crushes problems in one or two representations and loses points in the others.

The AP exam tests this relentlessly. You will see a free response question where a graph is your only input and you cannot compute a derivative directly. You will see MCQ where a table of values forces you to estimate an average rate of change and then decide whether an instantaneous rate of change is even defined at a given point.

My opinion: if your study plan does not specifically practice all four representations every week, you are preparing for the wrong exam.

## The 12-Week Plan {#the-12-week-plan}

This is the exact sequence I walk my private students through. The plan assumes you start in September or October, which gives you 12 weeks before the holiday break and a natural review window in the spring. Each phase has a primary focus, a secondary goal, and one key daily habit.

---

### Phase 1: Weeks 1-3 -- Limits and Continuity (Units 1-2)

**Primary focus:** Building the language of calculus from scratch.

Limits are not just a Unit 1 topic. They define what a derivative is, what an integral is, and why L'Hopital's Rule works. Students who rush past limits pay for it in every unit that follows. Every other concept in the course rests on what you do in these first three weeks.

**Week 1:** Learn the definition of a limit graphically and numerically. Practice limit tables. Know the one-sided limit idea cold. Be able to say, without hesitation, why $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x)$ can exist even when $f(2)$ does not.

**Week 2:** Algebraic limit techniques. Factor and cancel. Rationalize. The conjugate method for square root expressions. These appear on the MCQ section far more than students expect.

**Week 3:** Continuity. The three-part definition. Removable, jump, and infinite discontinuities. The Intermediate Value Theorem. Know what each type looks like on a graph and what it means algebraically.

**Daily habit (Phase 1):** Five MCQ limit problems every morning. Timed. Write down every technique you used and why.

---

### Phase 2: Weeks 4-6 -- Derivatives (Units 2-3)

**Primary focus:** Mastering every differentiation rule and getting fast at applying them.

The derivative rules are not something you "kind of" know. On exam day, they need to be automatic. Power rule, product rule, quotient rule, chain rule, trig derivatives, exponential and logarithm derivatives.

**Week 4:** Power rule and product rule without notes. Work 20 mixed problems per session. No calculator. No formula sheet.

**Week 5:** Chain rule. This is the rule that appears most often in composite function problems, and it is the rule students most often apply incorrectly. See Worked Example 1 below for exactly what correct execution looks like.

**Week 6:** Implicit differentiation. Introduction to related rates. Every related rates problem has a setup phase: draw the picture, write the equation relating the variables, differentiate both sides with respect to time. Practice the setup before you worry about the algebra.

**Daily habit (Phase 2):** One related rates problem per day. Draw the diagram before touching the math.

---

### Phase 3: Weeks 7-8 -- Applications of Derivatives (Units 4-5)

**Primary focus:** Using derivatives to answer questions, not just computing them.

This is where the average rate of change versus instantaneous rate of change distinction becomes a real scoring issue. Average rate of change is a slope calculation over an interval:

$$\frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$$

Instantaneous rate of change is the derivative evaluated at a single point: $f'(a)$. The AP exam tests whether you know which concept applies to the question being asked. Students who blur these two ideas lose points on both MCQ and FRQ.

**Week 7:** Mean Value Theorem. Extreme Value Theorem. First derivative test. Second derivative test. Know what each theorem requires before you can apply it.

**Week 8:** Curve sketching from derivative information. Concavity. Points of inflection. Be able to describe the behavior of $f$ when you are given a graph of $f'$.

**Daily habit (Phase 3):** One curve-sketching problem where you start from $f'$ and reason backward to $f$. These appear in FRQ Part B every year.

---

### Phase 4: Weeks 9-10 -- Integration (Unit 6)

**Primary focus:** Antiderivatives, definite integrals, and the Fundamental Theorem.

**Week 9:** Basic antiderivatives and u-substitution. U-sub is the integration version of the chain rule. If you know chain rule cold from Phase 2, u-sub will click faster than you expect.

**Week 10:** Definite integrals. Riemann sums (left, right, midpoint, trapezoidal). The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, both parts. Accumulation function problems.

**Daily habit (Phase 4):** One definite integral per day using the Fundamental Theorem. Write every step. Do not skip the evaluation at the bounds.

---

### Phase 5: Weeks 11-12 -- Exam Prep and Timed Practice

**Primary focus:** Simulating real exam conditions so the actual exam feels familiar.

This is the phase most students jump to in April when they are panicking. That is a mistake. If you have done Phases 1-4, these two weeks will feel completely different.

**Week 11:** Full timed MCQ section (45 questions, 105 minutes). Review every wrong answer. Categorize each mistake by topic.

**Week 12:** Full timed FRQ section (6 questions, 90 minutes). After each practice session, read the College Board scoring guidelines for those questions. If you lost points, find the exact reason.

**Daily habit (Phase 5):** Read one College Board scoring commentary per day. These documents tell you exactly what graders look for, in plain language.

## Worked Example 1: Chain Rule (What You Are Doing in Phase 2) {#worked-example-1}

Every student hitting Week 5 will encounter a chain rule problem that looks something like this. Here is how to work through it the way a grader wants to see it.

**Problem:** Find $f'(x)$ where $f(x) = \sin(3x^2 + 1)$.

---

**Step 1: Identify the structure.**

This is a composite function. The outer function is $\sin(\square)$ and the inner function is $3x^2 + 1$. Chain rule applies whenever you have a function of a function.

$$f(x) = \sin\!\bigl(3x^2 + 1\bigr)$$

Outer: $\sin(\square)$. Inner: $3x^2 + 1$.

---

**Step 2: Differentiate the outer function, keep the inner function inside.**

$$f'(x) = \cos(3x^2 + 1) \cdot \frac{d}{dx}\bigl[3x^2 + 1\bigr]$$

The $\cos$ appears because the derivative of $\sin(u)$ is $\cos(u)$. The inner function $3x^2 + 1$ stays unchanged inside the cosine.

---

**Step 3: Differentiate the inner function.**

$$\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[3x^2 + 1\bigr] = 6x$$

---

**Step 4: Multiply and write the complete answer.**

$$\boxed{f'(x) = 6x\cos(3x^2 + 1)}$$

---

> **Here is exactly how the AP exam scores this type of problem.**
>
> On a free response, the grader checks three things:
>
> - (1 point) Correct identification that chain rule is needed and correct setup
> - (1 point) Correct derivative of the outer function with the inner function preserved inside
> - (1 point) Correct derivative of the inner function multiplied through
>
> If you differentiate the outer function correctly but forget to multiply by the inner derivative, you lose a point. If you write $\cos(6x)$ instead of $\cos(3x^2 + 1)$, you lose a point. Both errors are extremely common in Week 5. The fix: always write out the composite structure before you differentiate, so you can see both layers clearly.

---

The single most common error I see in Week 5 is treating the argument of the trigonometric function as if it disappears after differentiation. It does not. The inner function stays inside the cosine, and then you multiply by its derivative. That is the complete chain rule procedure, and it applies to every composite function on the exam.

## Worked Example 2: Limit Recognition on MCQ (What You Are Mastering in Phase 1) {#worked-example-2}

This is the type of problem you drill in Phase 1. It looks simple. It trips students up because they do not recognize the form and reach for the wrong conclusion.

**Problem (MCQ style):**

What is $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3}$?

(A) 0 &nbsp;&nbsp; (B) 3 &nbsp;&nbsp; (C) 6 &nbsp;&nbsp; (D) The limit does not exist

---

**Recognition step: What form is this?**

Plug in $x = 3$ to check:

- Numerator: $3^2 - 9 = 0$
- Denominator: $3 - 3 = 0$

That is the $\frac{0}{0}$ indeterminate form. This is your signal. Do not stop here and pick (D). Indeterminate form means you need to simplify, not give up.

---

**Step 1: Factor the numerator.**

$$x^2 - 9 = (x + 3)(x - 3)$$

---

**Step 2: Cancel the common factor.**

$$\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{(x+3)(x-3)}{x-3} = \lim_{x \to 3}(x + 3)$$

This cancellation is valid because in a limit, $x$ approaches 3 but never equals 3, so $(x - 3) \neq 0$ throughout the process.

---

**Step 3: Evaluate.**

$$\lim_{x \to 3}(x + 3) = 3 + 3 = \boxed{6}$$

The answer is **(C) 6**.

---

> **Here is exactly what the AP exam is checking on this problem type.**
>
> The MCQ grader, and any FRQ rubric that involves a limit computation, is checking:
>
> - You recognized $\frac{0}{0}$ as indeterminate, not as "the limit does not exist"
> - You applied the correct algebraic technique (factor and cancel) to resolve the indeterminate form
> - You evaluated the simplified expression at the limit value correctly
>
> Students who pick (D) are applying the wrong rule. A limit does not exist when the one-sided limits disagree, or when the function grows without bound in opposite directions. It is not what happens when direct substitution gives $\frac{0}{0}$. That specific confusion costs points on 2-3 MCQ problems every year.

---

The pattern is recognize the form, choose the technique, execute. By the end of Week 2, I want students to reach for factor-and-cancel automatically whenever they see $\frac{0}{0}$ on an MCQ. The AP exam does not reward students who re-derive techniques under pressure. It rewards students who recognize familiar structures and respond without hesitation.

## The 3 Mistakes That Kill Your Score {#the-3-mistakes}

The three mistakes below account for the majority of the gap between a 4 and a 5. Students who avoid all three going into Phase 5 almost always end up in the 5 range.

### Mistake 1: Skipping Limits to Get to Derivatives Faster

Every year, I have at least one student who decides limits are "just the intro unit" and jumps to derivatives in Week 1. By Week 7, they cannot explain why the derivative is defined as a limit, they misapply L'Hopital's Rule when they should factor, and they freeze on any FRQ that asks about limit behavior at infinity.

Limits are not a warmup. They are the foundation every other concept in the course rests on.

### Mistake 2: Never Doing Timed Practice Until Week 11

Students who only practice without time pressure do not build exam pacing. On the AP exam, you have about 2 minutes per MCQ and 15 minutes per FRQ. If you have never practiced at that pace, the clock becomes a psychological obstacle on exam day.

The fix: start doing timed MCQ sets as early as Week 6. One timed set per week builds the pacing instinct without burning you out during skill-building phases.

### Mistake 3: Studying Topics Instead of Question Types

A student can finish every problem in their textbook's chain rule chapter and still miss chain rule questions on the AP exam. The reason: the AP presents topics in question-type wrappers that textbooks do not replicate. Composite function derivatives, implicit differentiation with respect to time, accumulation FRQ, no-calculator FRQ with only graph information -- these are different from textbook exercises.

Study the actual AP question types, not just the underlying math topics.

## Practice Problems {#practice-problems}

Work these without a calculator.

**Problem 1 (Phase 1 skill -- limits):**

Evaluate: $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -2} \frac{x^2 + 5x + 6}{x + 2}$

**Problem 2 (Phase 2 skill -- chain rule):**

Find $g'(x)$ where $g(x) = e^{4x^3 - x}$.

**Problem 3 (Phase 4 skill -- definite integral):**

Evaluate: $\displaystyle\int_{1}^{4} \bigl(3x^2 - 2x\bigr)\,dx$

---

**Answers:**

**Problem 1:** Factor the numerator: $(x + 2)(x + 3)/(x + 2) \to x + 3$. Evaluate: $-2 + 3 = \boxed{1}$

**Problem 2:** Chain rule with outer $e^u$ and inner $4x^3 - x$. Answer: $g'(x) = (12x^2 - 1)e^{4x^3 - x}$

**Problem 3:** Antiderivative: $x^3 - x^2$. Evaluate: $(64 - 16) - (1 - 1) = 48 - 0 = \boxed{48}$

## FAQ {#faq}

### How long does it take to prepare for a 5 on AP Calculus AB?

Most students need 10-14 weeks of consistent daily practice. The 12-week plan above assumes 45-60 minutes per day. Students who start in September have the strongest foundation because the limit and continuity phases do not feel rushed. Cramming the last three weeks of April is a plan that almost never produces a 5.

### Is AP Calculus AB harder than other AP exams?

It is demanding, but the scope is more contained than students expect. The exam covers six units, and roughly 40% of the MCQ section focuses on derivatives and their applications. If you build derivative fluency in Phase 2, you go into Phase 5 with a structural advantage on the biggest portion of the test.

### What is the best resource for AP Calculus AB FRQ practice?

The College Board website has every released free response question from 2012 through the most recent exam, with official scoring guidelines attached. Those documents are the single best practice resource available. I tell my students to save them for Phase 5. Before that, the focus should be building skills rather than simulating exam conditions.

### Do I need a tutor to score a 5 on AP Calculus AB?

No. This plan works with or without a tutor. What a tutor adds is faster diagnosis when you are stuck and accountability to the weekly schedule. If you are disciplined enough to move through all five phases consistently, the path to a 5 is open to you on your own.
