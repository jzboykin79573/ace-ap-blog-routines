You know what a "smiley face" graph looks like. You know what a "frown" looks like. And if that is all you have, you are one wrong MCQ answer away from wondering how you missed something you definitely learned.

Concavity is not a shape. It is a statement about how fast the slope is changing. That distinction is the entire difference between a student who recognizes a concavity problem on sight and one who draws a bowl with their pencil and hopes for the best.

The AP exam does not ask you to identify shapes. It asks you to use concavity as a tool. Specifically: to apply the Second Derivative Test to classify critical points, to determine whether a Riemann sum overestimates or underestimates, and to locate inflection points accurately. Three different question types. All rooted in the same concept. Most students only ever learn the bowl version.

I see it constantly. Students score 80s and 90s on unit tests, then freeze on the MCQ because the graph looks slightly different from the practice packet. That is the gap concavity creates when you learn it by shape instead of by rate.

Concavity is not a shape you memorize — it is a rate of change you can measure and use.

What Concavity Actually Means (Not the Bowl Shape)

Every AP student has heard "concave up is a smiley face." That gets you through a quiz. It will not get you through the exam.

Here is the real definition. A function is concave up on an interval when $f''(x) > 0$ on that interval. That means the first derivative $f'(x)$ is increasing. The slope is going up. If you imagined walking along the curve from left to right, the ground is tilting upward.

A function is concave down when $f''(x) < 0$. The first derivative $f'(x)$ is decreasing. The slope is going down.

The bowl analogy is a consequence of this, not the definition. A curve where slope keeps increasing is going to look like the bottom of a bowl. A curve where slope keeps decreasing is going to look like a hill. The visual comes from the math, not the other way around.

My take: Learn the bowl to double-check your answer. Do not learn the bowl to find the answer.

An inflection point is where the concavity changes. Not just where $f''(x) = 0$. Not just where $f''(x)$ does not exist. Those are candidates. An inflection point is confirmed only when $f''(x)$ changes sign at that $x$ value.

That one sentence is worth 3–4 MCQ points over the course of the exam. Write it down.

The AROC/IROC Frame: Why f''(x) Is the Real Story

You already know this setup. The derivative $f'(x)$ gives you the instantaneous rate of change of $f(x)$. It is the slope of the tangent line at a single point.

Now apply that same logic one level up.

$f''(x)$ is the derivative of $f'(x)$. That means $f''(x)$ is the instantaneous rate of change of $f'(x)$. In plain terms: $f''(x)$ tells you how fast the slope is changing at any given moment.

AROC of slope
$$\frac{f'(b) - f'(a)}{b - a}$$
Average rate at which slope changes over $[a, b]$
IROC of slope = f''(x)
$$\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f'(x+h)-f'(x)}{h}$$
Instantaneous rate at which slope is changing at $x$

Why does this matter? Because it reframes what concavity means in terms you already know.

  • Concave up: $f''(x) > 0$ means the IROC of slope is positive. Slope is growing. $f'(x)$ is increasing.
  • Concave down: $f''(x) < 0$ means the IROC of slope is negative. Slope is shrinking. $f'(x)$ is decreasing.

This is not just a conceptual note. It is directly tested on FRQ justifications. When the AP exam asks you to justify why a function is concave up on an interval, "the slope is increasing" is a scoring response. "It looks like a bowl" is not.

How to Find Concavity and Inflection Points: Step-by-Step

1
Find $f''(x)$

Take the derivative of the derivative. If you have $f'(x)$ already, differentiate it. Pay attention to signs.

2
Find where $f''(x) = 0$ or is undefined

These are the only $x$ values where concavity can change. Call them candidates.

3
Build a sign chart for $f''(x)$

Pick test values in each interval. Plug into $f''(x)$. Positive means concave up. Negative means concave down.

4
Confirm inflection points with sign change

An inflection point exists at $x = c$ only if $f''(x)$ changes sign on either side of $c$. If the sign does not change, $c$ is not an inflection point, even if $f''(c) = 0$.

5
Apply the Second Derivative Test (when classifying critical points)

If $f'(c) = 0$, apply the SDT:

Second Derivative Test
$f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) > 0$
Local minimum
$f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) < 0$
Local maximum
$f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) = 0$
Inconclusive
Use First Derivative Test

That last case is the one students skip. The AP exam gives you functions designed to test it.

Worked Example 1: Full Concavity Analysis on $f(x) = x^4 - 4x^3$

Let's work through $f(x) = x^4 - 4x^3$ completely. This function has both inflection points and a critical point where the Second Derivative Test fails. It is exactly the kind of function that shows up on free-response questions.

1
Find $f'(x)$
$$f'(x) = 4x^3 - 12x^2 = 4x^2(x - 3)$$

Critical points at $x = 0$ and $x = 3$.

2
Find $f''(x)$
$$f''(x) = 12x^2 - 24x = 12x(x - 2)$$

$f''(x) = 0$ at $x = 0$ and $x = 2$. These are candidates for inflection points.

3
Sign chart for $f''(x)$
Interval Test value $f''$ value Concavity
$x < 0$ $x = -1$ $12(-1)(-3) = 36 > 0$ Concave up
$0 < x < 2$ $x = 1$ $12(1)(-1) = -12 < 0$ Concave down
$x > 2$ $x = 3$ $12(3)(1) = 36 > 0$ Concave up
4
Confirm inflection points

At $x = 0$: $f''$ changes sign from positive to negative. Sign change confirmed. Inflection point at $(0, 0)$.

At $x = 2$: $f''$ changes sign from negative to positive. Sign change confirmed. Inflection point at $(2, 2^4 - 4(2)^3) = (2, 16 - 32) = (2, -16)$.

5
Apply SDT at the critical points

At $x = 0$: $f'(0) = 0$ and $f''(0) = 0$. The SDT is inconclusive.

Back to the First Derivative Test. Look at $f'(x) = 4x^2(x-3)$ on either side of $x = 0$:

  • $x = -0.5$: $f'(-0.5) = 4(0.25)(-3.5) = -3.5 < 0$
  • $x = 0.5$: $f'(0.5) = 4(0.25)(-2.5) = -2.5 < 0$

Same sign on both sides. No sign change. $x = 0$ is not a local extremum.

At $x = 3$: $f'(3) = 0$ and $f''(3) = 12(3)(1) = 36 > 0$. Local minimum at $x = 3$. The SDT works here.

AP Scoring: Here is exactly how the AP exam scores this type of problem
  • Point 1 -- Correct computation of $f''(x)$: show your algebra clearly. Do not skip differentiation steps.
  • Point 2 -- Inflection point identification: you must show the sign chart or equivalent evidence that $f''(x)$ changes sign. Writing "$f''(x) = 0$ at $x = 2$" alone does not earn the justification point.
  • Point 3 -- Classification at $x = 3$: state $f''(3) > 0$ and therefore local min. If SDT was inconclusive, the grader expects FDT work in its place.
Common partial credit trap: students earn Points 1 and 3 but lose Point 2 because they list inflection points without sign change evidence. One sentence with labeled test values is all you need.

Worked Example 2: SDT, Inflection Points, and Riemann Sum Connection (FRQ Level)

This is the version that shows up on the actual exam. It connects 3 ideas in one problem. I see this tested almost every year in some form.

Problem setup: Let $g(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x + 1$.

(a) Find all intervals where $g$ is concave up and concave down.
(b) Identify all inflection points. Justify.
(c) A student approximates $\int_0^2 g(x)\,dx$ using a Midpoint Riemann sum with 2 equal subintervals. Is this an overestimate or underestimate? Justify.

Part (a): Concavity Intervals

$$g'(x) = 3x^2 - 12x + 9$$
$$g''(x) = 6x - 12 = 6(x - 2)$$

$g''(x) = 0$ at $x = 2$.

Interval Test value $g''$ value Concavity
$x < 2$ $x = 1$ $6(1) - 12 = -6 < 0$ Concave down on $(-\infty, 2)$
$x > 2$ $x = 3$ $6(3) - 12 = 6 > 0$ Concave up on $(2, \infty)$

Part (b): Inflection Point

At $x = 2$, $g''$ changes sign from negative to positive. Inflection point confirmed.

$$g(2) = 8 - 24 + 18 + 1 = 3$$

Inflection point at $(2, 3)$.

Part (c): Midpoint Riemann Sum -- Overestimate or Underestimate?

On $[0, 2]$, a Midpoint sum with 2 equal subintervals uses $x = 0.5$ and $x = 1.5$ as sample points (midpoints of $[0,1]$ and $[1,2]$).

$$g(0.5) = (0.5)^3 - 6(0.5)^2 + 9(0.5) + 1 = 0.125 - 1.5 + 4.5 + 1 = 4.125$$
$$g(1.5) = (1.5)^3 - 6(1.5)^2 + 9(1.5) + 1 = 3.375 - 13.5 + 13.5 + 1 = 4.375$$
$$M_2 = g(0.5)(1) + g(1.5)(1) = 4.125 + 4.375 = 8.5$$

Now, is 8.5 an overestimate or underestimate?

On $[0, 2]$: $g''(x) = 6x - 12 < 0$ for all $x < 2$. The function is concave down on the entire interval $[0, 2]$.

Concavity Trapezoidal Rule Midpoint Rule
Concave up ($f'' > 0$) Overestimates Underestimates
Concave down ($f'' < 0$) Underestimates Overestimates

Since $g$ is concave down on $[0, 2]$, the Midpoint sum of 8.5 is an overestimate.

Why? On a concave down curve, the tangent line at any point lies above the curve. The Midpoint rectangle uses the function height at the center of each subinterval. That height corresponds to a point on the curve, but the curve is bending away from the rectangles. The rectangles stick up above the curve.

AP Scoring: Here is exactly what the AP exam is checking on this problem type
  • Part (a) -- You need $g''(x)$ computed correctly and a sign analysis with stated intervals in interval notation.
  • Part (b) -- The key phrase: "g''(x) changes from negative to positive at x = 2, so there is an inflection point at (2, 3)." State both coordinates.
  • Part (c) -- Cite concavity as justification: "Since $g$ is concave down on $[0,2]$, the Midpoint Riemann sum overestimates the integral." That sentence is the complete scoring justification. Showing arithmetic is not required for this point, but it demonstrates command of the method.
Partial credit note: many students earn Parts (a) and (b) but write "the sum is larger than the actual area" for Part (c) without citing concavity. That is not a scoring justification on its own.

The 3 Mistakes That Cost AP Points

Mistake 1: Calling f''(c) = 0 an inflection point without checking sign change
Why it happens: The procedure says "set f'' equal to zero," so students stop there.
Setting $f''(x) = 0$ gives candidates, not conclusions. You must verify the sign of $f''$ on both sides. If the sign does not change, there is no inflection point at that $x$ value.
Consequence: You list an inflection point that does not exist, lose the justification point, and possibly misclassify a nearby critical point. One gap, three point losses.
Mistake 2: Stopping at "f''(c) = 0, test inconclusive" without running the FDT
Why it happens: Students learn the SDT, get an inconclusive result, and consider the problem finished.
When $f''(c) = 0$, you get zero information from the SDT. You must follow up with the First Derivative Test. Examine the sign of $f'$ on each side of $x = c$. The AP exam designs problems like $f(x) = x^4 - 4x^3$ specifically to catch students who stop early.
Consequence: A student who writes "SDT inconclusive" and moves on earns zero on that scoring point. A student who runs the FDT earns the point.
Mistake 3: Flipping the Riemann sum rule for concavity
Why it happens: There are two methods (Trapezoidal and Midpoint) and two directions (over/under), and they go in opposite directions for each type of concavity.
I get this wrong too when I rush. The clean version: concave up means Trapezoidal overestimates and Midpoint underestimates. Concave down flips both. Write it on your formula sheet and keep the table above open during practice.
Name the feeling: this is the mistake that hits on the last MCQ of a section when you are tired. You know the connection exists but you flip the direction. Costs one question, which on this exam matters.

Practice Problems

Three problems. Work them before reading the answers.

Problem 1

Let $h(x) = x^3 - 3x^2 - 9x + 5$.

Find all inflection points of $h$. Justify your answer.

Problem 2

Let $f(x) = x^4 - 8x^2$.

(a) Find all intervals where $f$ is concave up and concave down.

(b) Use the Second Derivative Test to classify each critical point. If the SDT is inconclusive at a critical point, state that and use the FDT instead.

Problem 3 (MCQ style)

The function $k$ is twice differentiable and $k''(x) > 0$ for all $x$ in $[1, 4]$. A student uses the Trapezoidal Rule with 3 equal subintervals to approximate $\int_1^4 k(x)\,dx$. Which of the following is true?

(A) The approximation is an overestimate.
(B) The approximation is an underestimate.
(C) The approximation equals the exact value.
(D) The relationship cannot be determined.

Answers
Problem 1: $h''(x) = 6x - 6 = 6(x - 1)$. Zero at $x = 1$. Sign check: $h''(0) = -6 < 0$, $h''(2) = 6 > 0$. Sign changes. Inflection point at $x = 1$. $h(1) = 1 - 3 - 9 + 5 = -6$. Inflection point at $(1, -6)$.
Problem 2: $f'(x) = 4x^3 - 16x = 4x(x-2)(x+2)$. Critical points: $x = -2, 0, 2$. $f''(x) = 12x^2 - 16$. Zeros at $x = \pm\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \approx \pm 1.155$. Concave up on $(-\infty, -1.155)$ and $(1.155, \infty)$. Concave down on $(-1.155, 1.155)$. SDT: $f''(-2) = 32 > 0$ (local min). $f''(0) = -16 < 0$ (local max). $f''(2) = 32 > 0$ (local min).
Problem 3: (A) $k''(x) > 0$ on $[1, 4]$ means $k$ is concave up. The Trapezoidal Rule overestimates when the function is concave up.

FAQ

Q Can a function have f''(c) = 0 without having an inflection point at x = c? +

Yes. $f(x) = x^4$ is the classic example. $f''(x) = 12x^2$, so $f''(0) = 0$. But $f''(x) > 0$ on both sides of $x = 0$. No sign change. No inflection point. In my experience, the AP exam tests this exact scenario at least once per exam year, usually in MCQ Section I. Always check the sign of $f''$ on both sides before calling anything an inflection point.

Q How do I justify an inflection point on the FRQ? +

Two things. First, state that $f''(c) = 0$ (or does not exist). Second, show that $f''$ changes sign at $x = c$ by evaluating $f''$ on both sides. A sign chart with labeled test values is accepted. One sentence like "Since $f''$ changes from negative to positive at $x = 2$, there is an inflection point at $x = 2$" earns the justification point. You also need to state the coordinates of the inflection point, not just the $x$ value.

Q When does the Second Derivative Test fail? +

It fails when $f''(c) = 0$ at a critical point. At that point, you get no information from the SDT. Use the First Derivative Test instead: examine the sign of $f'$ on each side of $x = c$. If $f'$ changes from negative to positive, local min. If positive to negative, local max. If no sign change, neither. The function $f(x) = x^4 - 4x^3$ at $x = 0$ is the worked example of this in the post above.

Q Does the Midpoint Rule always underestimate? +

No. That is a very common misconception. The Midpoint Rule underestimates only when the function is concave up. It overestimates when the function is concave down. If concavity changes over the interval, you cannot make a general claim without more analysis. This distinction is tested directly on AP MCQ questions, usually with a function whose concavity is stated explicitly in the problem.

Quick Reference: Concavity and Inflection Points
Concavity rule

$f''(x) > 0$ on an interval → concave up (slope increasing)
$f''(x) < 0$ on an interval → concave down (slope decreasing)

Inflection point confirmation

$f''(c) = 0$ (or undefined) AND $f''$ changes sign at $x = c$

Second Derivative Test

$f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) > 0$ → local min
$f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) < 0$ → local max
$f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) = 0$ → inconclusive, use FDT

Riemann sums and concavity

Concave up: Trap overestimates, Midpoint underestimates
Concave down: Trap underestimates, Midpoint overestimates

  • Inflection point requires sign change in $f''$, not just $f''(c) = 0$
  • $f''(x)$ = IROC of slope = rate of change of $f'(x)$
  • SDT inconclusive when $f''(c) = 0$ at a critical point
  • State both coordinates $(x, y)$ for every inflection point on the FRQ
  • Never call $f''(c) = 0$ an inflection point without a sign chart
  • Never stop at "SDT inconclusive" without running the FDT
